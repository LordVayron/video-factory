# Sitcom writing bible (sitcoms and comedy series only)

A distilled craft reference from research into 90s sitcom writing. Read it before writing any sitcom line, and score every draft against the checklist at the bottom. Do not apply it to other verticals.

## Character and story
- **The engine is the character's flaw, not a gag.** Give each character one fixed flaw that drives their choices: vain and power-hungry, literal and food-driven, oblivious, accidentally right.
- **The trap:** the characters are stuck together (an apartment, an office, a lease, a family), so friction never ends.
- **Proportionate jeopardy:** a trivial thing (a sandwich, a cardigan, a parking spot) carries Greek-tragedy weight for someone's ego.
- **No hugging, no learning.** The status quo is restored every episode.
- **Escalate logically within personality.** No out-of-character behaviour for a cheap laugh. Ask: "Would this character actually say that?"
- **Asymmetrical information is a farce engine.** When the audience knows something a character doesn't (a secret, a disguise, animals who talk only to each other), exploit the gap in every scene: someone unknowingly says the truth, the secret nearly comes out.

## Joke mechanics
- **Rule of three:** two items set a pattern, the third shatters it. Rhythm is short, short, LONG. Two items feel unfinished; four feel like a list.
- **Word economy:** the setup is as short as grammar allows, and the punchline snaps like a whip. Cut adjectives, adverbs and transitions.
- **No shoe-leather:** no "let me explain", no greetings, no exposition lines. Every sentence is a setup or a punchline.
- **Hard consonants (K, C, Q, T, P, G, D, B):** do a phonetic audit and swap soft words for hard ones (bucks over dollars, ketchup over sauce, cupcake over sweetie). **The last word of every punchline ends on a hard stop.**
- **Callbacks:** plant a setup, phrase or prop early and pay it off at the end. The final punchline should recall the opening premise (bookend).
- **Reactions are jokes:** cut from the punchline to the listener's face. A silent reaction can be the laugh.
- **Density:** 90s shows ran about 3–3.6 jokes per minute. **A 60 s short needs at least 5–7 distinct comedic beats.**
- **Catchphrases are never forced.** Running bits grow from character.

## 60-second structure
| Time | Beat | Job |
|---|---|---|
| 0–3 s | Cold open, in media res | The first line states the trap and the disproportionate jeopardy. No greeting. |
| 0–15 s | Setup (beat 1) | Establish the pattern and the premise. |
| 15–30 s | Reinforcement (beat 2) | Repeat and raise the stakes slightly. |
| 30–45 s | Subversion (beat 3) | Shatter the pattern; the absurd or unexpected main laugh. |
| 45–60 s | Callback / button | Rapid callback to the opening premise; ends on a hard consonant. |
- **Subplots become background gags:** a character doing something absurd in the background while the dialogue plays in front.
- **Ending:** the final word is a hard plosive, and the edit **smash-cuts on that consonant**. No lingering wide.

## Editing and cadence
- Multi-cam coverage: master wide, two-shots, singles, and dedicated reaction close-ups. Never move the camera for its own sake.
- In short form there is no holding for laughs: deliver the punchline, then cut to the next setup.
- A wide static master is allowed for physical comedy (let the body language play).

## Classic scene mechanics to borrow (never lines)
- **The escalating lie:** a lie that grows with every line while the skeptic keeps accepting the absurd.
- **The callback prop:** an object planted early resolves two threads in one physical reveal.
- **"They don't know that we know":** rapid reveals and a competitive refusal to yield.
- **The double meaning:** one conversation carries two different meanings for two characters, while doors open and close on cue.
- **The archetype subverted physically:** the stiff character suddenly dances.

## Checklist (every sitcom script must pass before the user sees it)
- [ ] The first line is in media res and states the trap or jeopardy.
- [ ] 5–7+ distinct comedic beats in 60 s (count them, list them).
- [ ] At least one rule-of-three with a real third-beat twist.
- [ ] Every line is a setup or a punchline (no shoe-leather).
- [ ] Phonetic audit done; every punchline ends on a hard consonant.
- [ ] A callback: the ending pays off something planted in the first 15 s.
- [ ] Any information asymmetry the show has is used and SHOWN on screen.
- [ ] Every character acts in character.
- [ ] The button is visible and makes sense on screen with no off-screen sound needed. A sound may support a gag but never carry it alone.
- [ ] Stage geography and eyelines are consistent (see the series room map).
