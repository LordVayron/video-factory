# Vertical DNA: 90s multi-camera sitcom

Read this before writing, boarding, filming or editing any sitcom. It is the bar. **Funny dialogue is the priority over everything else, including the picture.** Score every script with `sitcom-writing-bible.md`.

## 1. What made the great 90s sitcoms work

- **Character is the engine; the situation resets.** Every character has a fixed want and a fixed flaw that never gets fixed. Seinfeld's rule was "no hugging, no learning". Characters do not grow, they repeat their flaw in a new situation. ([Screen Rant](https://screenrant.com/seinfeld-no-hugging-learning-rule-darkest-sitcom-1990s/))
- **Straight man against eccentric.**
  - The straight man keeps composure while the eccentric behaves absurdly, and most laughs come from the straight man's reaction. ([Straight man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Straight_man))
  - Frasier added the **pompous character whose vanity gets punctured** by the people around them. ([No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/top-character-archetypes-in-comedy))
  - A reliable trio: **the pompous schemer** (straight man to the chaos, and the target when the plan collapses), **the innocent eccentric** (literal, sweet questions that are accidentally the smartest thing said), and **the deflator** (dry, amused, the only adult in the room).
- **Joke density.**
  - The multi-cam target was about **3 jokes per script page**, built as hard set-up and punchline jokes. ([Schettini](https://www.antonschettini.com/post/tv-writing-101-multi-cam-comedy-vs-single-cam-comedy))
  - Shows ran at about **3 laugh lines per minute** over a 22-minute episode. ([HuffPost](https://www.huffpost.com/entry/i-bingewatched-9-seasons-of-seinfeld_b_6834722))
  - A short has no time to warm up, so run denser: **one laugh every 5–7 s, at least 4 in a 30 s short.**
- **Every joke serves the story.** Punchlines pay off an earlier setup or tie threads together. Insult-for-insult's-sake is the cheap version.
- **Rooted in reality, dressed funny.** Find a real, relatable dilemma first, then dress it up the funniest way possible. Story first, then jokes, then the room punches it up.
- **Structure.**
  - **Cold open:** a short, self-contained laugh scene before the titles.
  - **A story and B story.**
  - **Runner:** a joke repeated about three times across the episode.
  - **Tag:** one final joke after the story ends. ([Cold open](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_open))
  - **A 30 s short is a cold open:** one situation, one game, one button.
- **Scene economy.** Cut to a new scene about every minute; no scene outstays its joke.

## 2. Joke toolkit (use at least three different ones per short)

| Tool | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Set-up / punch split across two characters | One character sets up, the other lands it, with the punchword last | A: "Can I be the boss?" B: "...You are the intern." |
| Misdirection | The line sets up one reading, then reveals another | "We need one more thing... a company." |
| Rule of three | Two normal items, then a third that breaks the pattern | "A yacht. A penthouse. A second hamster." |
| Literal-minded answer | The eccentric answers the literal words, not the meaning | "We need capital." "Like... Paris?" |
| Punctured pomposity | A grand statement immediately deflated | The big speech, then someone walks in with a vacuum |
| Topper | A second, bigger joke on top of the first laugh | "You know the mailman." "He LOVES me." |
| Callback / runner | A line from earlier (or another episode) pays off later | The same question asked a third time at the worst moment |
| Specificity | Exact, concrete nouns are funnier than general ones | "the mailman", not "a guy" |
| Reaction as punchline | A silent face lands the joke; cut to the listener | A slow blink |

Line rules:
- **Punchword at the very end of the line.** Never explain a joke after the punchword.
- **Lines are short:** 3–12 words. Long speeches only when the length itself is the joke, and then they get deflated.
- **No exposition lines.** Every line is a set-up, a punch, or a character beat that sets up the next punch.
- **Each line is in its owner's voice.** You can't swap two characters' lines.
- Hard consonants and concrete words land better ("k" sounds, numbers, brand-free specifics).

## 3. Performance and picture grammar (multi-cam)

- **Coverage:** 3–4 cameras on one side of the set (proscenium), never crossing the line: a wide master, two-shots and eye-level singles. Cameras are locked off. The only allowed move is a slow push-in for a dramatic beat.
- **Cuts:** on the speaker change; to the listener for the reaction laugh; average shot about 2–4 s. A shot with nothing new in it is a dead shot.
- **Sets:** high-key, warm, lived-in, with one fixed home set. Night scenes keep the lamp-lit look in every shot (lighting lock).
- **Performances:** big but grounded. Double takes, slow burns, deadpan stares and held looks to camera are allowed. Animal characters stay natural animals whose faces do the acting.

## 4. Sound and timing

- **Pace:** dialogue at **150–190 wpm**; gaps between lines **0.1–0.4 s**; a deliberate beat before a punchline **0.6–1.0 s, max one or two per short**. A silence longer than about 1 s with no visual change loses viewers.
- **Laugh track** (real editors trimmed generous audiences): chuckle 0.8–1.2 s, laugh 1.5–2.5 s, big laugh max 3 s and only on the button. The next line starts while the laugh decays, and the laugh ducks under it. Never wait for a laugh to finish.
- **No music under dialogue.** A sting goes between scenes and on the button; an outro riff can ride the freeze frame.

## 5. Short-form layer (vertical feed)

- **First 2–3 s:** the first line already contains the absurd premise or a joke. No establishing silence, no title card.
- **First laugh by 6 s.** A second hook (a bigger reversal) around 12–15 s.
- **Captions on:** much of the feed watches muted, so the joke must read in text too.
- **Length:** 40–60 s of episode (plus a short series intro if the show has one). End on the button plus a short tag, never on a lingering wide.

## 6. Writers' room procedure (every sitcom script, including the user's own)

1. **Premise:** the want, the obstacle, and the game ("the genius's grand plan versus the idiot's one sensible question").
2. **Beat map:** hook → escalate ×2 → reversal → button → tag.
3. **First draft at density:** at least one laugh line per 5–7 s.
4. **Punch-up pass:** for every line, funny, needed or cut; specific words; punchword last; a topper on the best laugh.
5. **Character pass:** every line belongs to its speaker.
6. **Table read:** generate the voices (`radio.mjs`), listen at real speed, cut anything that drags.
7. **Score with the rubric:** premise, game, lines, character, button, pace; every criterion ≥ 8. **When the user wrote the script:** keep their premise and best lines, and offer the punched version side by side.

## 7. Pacing QC (hard gate before the final cut)

| Metric | Pass |
|---|---|
| Words per minute (dialogue only) | ≥ 140 |
| Longest silence without a laugh or visual action | ≤ 0.6 s (planned beat ≤ 1.0 s) |
| Dead air in total (silences ≥ 0.4 s; turn-taking gaps don't count) | ≤ 8 % of runtime |
| First laugh | ≤ 6 s |
| Laugh lines | ≥ 1 per 7 s |
| Every scripted line present and correct (transcript) | 100 % |
| Ends on the button (+ freeze or tag ≤ 1.5 s) | yes |

## 8. Production lessons (learned the expensive way)

- **Audio first.** Shot durations guessed before the audio exists produce slow cuts. The table-read radio cut sets every duration.
- **Video models do literally what the prompt says about time.** Never write "holds still for N seconds" or ask for silence. Put the beat in the radio cut instead.
- ElevenLabs v3 reads slowly: `pace.tempo` around 1.1 plus inner pauses capped at 0.2 s reaches about 175 wpm.
- Seedance 2 Mini 480p follows multi-shot timing and lines well; the auto edit removes model-added air. It sometimes plays a planned close-up as the wide; that's acceptable when the line lands.
- **Seats drift.** Large animals and seated characters drift to the floor in video models, and close-ups show an empty cushion. Fix: scene `seats`, a SEAT LOCK in every prompt, dirty singles, and `continuity.mjs`. Any change of place needs an on-screen transition shot.
- **Physical punchlines are not silence.** The edit keeps the silence after the last line of a shot with `moves` (up to 2.5 s) so a leap or exit isn't trimmed.
- The continuity judge needs dense frames on moving shots and the start-of-shot state, or it calls planned exits "teleports".
- **Whispers convert badly with speech-to-speech** (they come out as another voice). Lines whose delivery says whisper use the clean radio-cut TTS, fitted to the on-screen duration.
- **Show, don't imply.** An ending that relies on an off-screen sound reads as nothing. The button must be visible.

## 9. Series

- A show lives in `series/<id>/`: `series.json` (cast, `roomMap`, voices, running bits, intro spec, ending, episodes), brand logo, character sheets, plates, the intro and its sounds. See `series/_example/series.json`.
- Episodes set `"series": "<id>"` in the shotlist; `edit.mjs` opens with the intro and dissolves into scene 1. `series.mjs intro-assets <id>` then `series.mjs intro <id>` builds it once.
- **Laughs v2** (`bible/sfx/v2`, generated on first use): 3 takes of a studio-audience prompt, the most sitcom-like envelope wins, a second take layered for crowd size with a TV band-pass, and the audience reacts 0.12–0.15 s after the punchword.

## 10. Scene flow: situations, not lists

- **Never structure a scene or a joke as "step one / step two / phase X".** It kills the flow and becomes a formula. Show the ambition through the situation and the arguing.
- **Rotate the engine per episode:**
  - **Small argument that escalates** (Seinfeld): a tiny grievance becomes a matter of principle.
  - **Pomposity meets farce** (Frasier): a dignified front punctured by small physical realities.
  - **The entrance gag** (Friends, Cheers): someone walks in mid-scheme and the conspirators "act natural", badly.
  - **The misunderstanding:** one means one thing, the other hears another, and both keep going.
  - **Animal reality** (for pet characters): instinct beats the plan; the physical gag is the punchline.
- **Cadence:** volleys of short overlapping lines. Reactions carry laughs as much as lines. A scene turns at least once.
- **Running bits** are spices: at most one per episode, never the button twice in a row.
- **The button** is a surprise rooted in character, ideally physical or a reversal, never a stock catchphrase.
